How I built Cosminova

A finished screenshot hides every wrong turn behind it. This page is the wrong turns: what the first versions looked like, what broke, what the first fix got wrong, and the tests that now keep each fix in place. Most stories begin with something I noticed was wrong on screen and end with a way to measure it.

Timeline

  1. 26 Aug 2026Started: a realistic night sky you could zoom into, seen from Earth.
  2. 5 Sep 2026First public build — the true-scale renderer, a Windows app, this website, and a native macOS shell.
  3. 6 Sep 2026iPhone and iPad builds; every star given a system; exoplanets given their own faces.
  4. 15 Oct 2026The Windows crash at launch fixed. An infinite universe: Gaia's 1.1 million stars, generated galaxies, and star systems invented on demand.
  5. 16 Oct 2026Precision far from home, click any point of light, a piano soundtrack, and varied galaxies, black holes and ringed giants.

Then and now

1. The 65,000-kilometre grid

Problem
Orbiting a star in a galaxy ten megaparsecs away, the star shook back and forth by six pixels as the camera turned, and its moons were drawn about 66 pixels from where they belonged.
First attempt
Positions were stored in kilometres from the Sun as 64-bit doubles — the standard approach, and fine at home. Moving the world so the camera sat at the origin (a "floating origin") steadied the near scene, but the shaking stayed.
What I learned
A double has 52 bits of fraction, so the gap between neighbouring numbers grows with the number. At 3 × 1020 km the gap is 65,536 km. Every position out there snaps to that grid, and subtracting two snapped positions — camera minus planet — can be wrong by a whole grid step, which is larger than most moons.
Solution
Never subtract two big numbers. Each planet and moon now stores its exact offset from its own star, and anything drawn near the camera is worked out from those small vectors. Camera flights were rebuilt the same way, as an offset from the target rather than a position in space.
Result
The shake fell from ±6 pixels to under ±0.5, and moons sit where their orbits put them.

2. The black moons

Problem
In an invented system, clicking any moon of a giant planet left the screen completely black. The camera had arrived inside the moon.
First attempt
The flight was rebuilt with the precision fix from the story above, and an automated check confirmed the camera now stopped at 3.4 moon radii instead of 0.002. The screenshots were still black: average brightness 0.6 out of 255.
What I learned
A fix can be right and still not be the fix. Reading the moon's shader inputs showed its sunlight was 0.0000016 of normal. Exposure was set for the light at the moon's orbit around its planet — a thousandth of an astronomical unit — instead of its distance from the star, so the camera expected a million times too much light. And moons behind their planet were approached from the night side.
Solution
Expose for the distance from the star, and when the usual framing would show mostly the dark side, come in from the lit side instead.
Result
Sunlight back to 1.0, average brightness 17–44, every moon tested arriving lit and outside.

3. Click any point of light

Problem
Inside Andromeda the sky is full of stars, but they were only paint: clicking one did nothing. I wanted every dot to be a place.
First attempt
Making invented stars clickable the same way as catalogue stars. The test click at the edge of Andromeda picked "Hamburger eta" — a generated star that was not drawn anywhere, but had no catalogue brightness, so nothing filtered it out.
What I learned
Picking has to agree exactly with drawing. If the click logic and the shader disagree about which dots are visible, you get clicks on nothing and misses on things you can plainly see.
Solution
On a click, every kind of point light is checked with the same brightness formula the shaders use, and the brightest within 11 pixels wins. A generated star becomes a full system — planets, moons, names — from a seed hashed from its position, so the same dot is always the same place.
Result
Clicking a star inside Andromeda flies you to it in about 40 milliseconds of work; a far smudge becomes a galaxy you can enter.

4. Tests that look at the screen

Problem
A universe has too many places to check by hand, and a fix in one place breaks another: changing what the click function returned silently broke spacecraft selection.
First attempt
Flying around after every change. It was slow, and it missed exactly the things that mattered, like a planet ten megaparsecs away that I had not happened to visit.
What I learned
Most of my judgements about the picture can be turned into numbers — brightness, how far the camera ended up, how much a star moves between frames, whether a click landed on what was under the cursor.
Solution
Over forty scripts drive the real app in headless Chrome. One visits every kind of destination; one sweeps the camera through a full 360° and fails on any jump; one scrolls from the ground to the surface of Vega without stopping; another flies to every moon of a far system and measures where it arrived and how bright it is.
Result
The spacecraft check caught the broken click the first time it ran, with the message "picked [object Object]".

Other mistakes worth keeping

  • Galaxies as white slabs. Small galaxies were drawn as flat bright rectangles until they became clouds of sprites with dust and star-forming knots.
  • A Windows app that crashed at launch. The first installer opened to nothing on some machines.
  • Two Betelgeuses. Two star catalogues both contained it, so it was drawn twice.
  • Words all over space. Galaxy names floated in open space by default; now the sky is quiet until you ask.
  • The App Store's sandbox. The Mac app had to be rebuilt to run sandboxed before Apple would review it.

Variety, the newest change

Invented galaxies used to share one model per type. They now vary the way real ones do: two to six arms, tight or loose winding, grand-design or patchy, big bulge or none, rings and ring galaxies, lopsided discs, shells around ellipticals, starbursts and quenched red discs. Black holes vary in disc size, temperature and how well fed they are, and some giant planets now have rings.

The test suite

A selection of the checks, and what each one fails on:

  • travel — every kind of destination is reachable and arrives lit.
  • orbit-360 — a full turn of the camera never jumps more than 5°.
  • zoom — one scroll from the ground reaches a star's surface without stalling.
  • roam — leaving free flight does not throw the camera across the system.
  • craft — every spacecraft can be picked, and none leaves stale entries behind.
  • system — a host star knows its galaxy and its planets.
  • site — the download links point at a real file, and support and privacy are reachable.
